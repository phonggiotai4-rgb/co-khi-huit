POSTED BY: MARCUS WATT · MAY 14
Drones: toys or tools?

How many readers recognize this photo of a mother bear and her cub? Lots of people saw this cute animal on social media, climbing a difficult mountain to reach its mother. The video's message seemed clear: success is possible if you keep trying. But later some scientists explained that the bears were afraid of the drone that was filming them, and that is why they made the dangerous climb.

There was also some news of drones causing problems when an airport in London was closed because a drone was flying too close. Just a few minutes with "a toy" stopped air traffic at one of the world's busiest airports and affected thousands of passengers. We don't know how much money it cost, but these stories are giving drones a bad name.

However, drones are easy to control, and they only need a little energy to fly, so the technology has lots of useful applications. Scientists can use drones to study wildlife in places that are difficult for humans to visit, such as Antarctica. And from California to Australia, drones have helped fight forest fires by showing firefighters what is happening from the sky. In emergency situations, drones can even deliver food and medical supplies.

As we've seen, drones can be good and bad. But are there any laws to control how people use them? The short answer is there are very few laws. Sadly, without regulation, some people will continue to use drones irresponsibly. When cars first appeared, there weren't any rules for the road, but now there is lots of traffic, and we understand that rules for drivers are necessary. Drones are no different, so let's make sure they become the safe technology of the twenty-first century.

8.1

-ing form and infinitive

When we use two verbs together, the second verb can be in the -ing form or infinitive:

I enjoy learning English. · I decided to learn English.

Verbs followed by infinitive (plans/intentions):

  • decide: I decided to write a blog.
  • agree: We agreed to help her.
  • hope: She's hoping to study music next year.
  • learn: I'm learning to play the piano.
  • manage: I managed to find a place with a backyard.
  • plan: They're planning to buy a new house.
  • prefer: Do you prefer to read the news online?
  • promise: They've promised to visit me.
  • want: He doesn't want to go to the festival.
  • would like: I'd like to meet new people.

Verbs followed by -ing form (emotions):

  • enjoy: She enjoys making her own clothes.
  • hate: I hate doing the ironing.
  • look forward to: He's looking forward to starting the job.
  • not mind: I don't mind waiting for the bus.
  • recommend: He recommended taking the train.
  • spend time: They spent a lot of time shopping.
  • can't stand: I can't stand doing the laundry.
  • suggest: He suggested changing my major.
  • finish: I've finished writing my essay.
  • miss: She really misses seeing her friends.

We can use some verbs, including like, love, prefer, start, and continue, in either form without any change in meaning:

It has started to rain. = It has started raining. · He continued to work. = He continued working.

Understanding inference and purpose

AI: amazing invention or awful idea?

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is a term we hear a lot these days, and people love to make comparisons with the early robots made popular by science fiction. In 1968, the movie 2001: A Space Odyssey introduced the world to HAL, an intelligent computer that takes control of a spaceship against the wishes of the astronauts. In fact, the word robot first appeared in 1921, in a play by the Czech writer Karel Čapek about machines taking over the world. So what, actually, is AI, and should we be worried?

Traditionally, people programmed computers to follow strict rules to do specific tasks. However, things changed in 2015. A team of computer scientists wanted to create a computer program that could play Go, a board game that has been played in Asia for over 2,500 years. But rather than writing rules telling the computer how to play well, they let the computer analyze thousands of games of Go and "learn" how to play well. The result was AlphaGo, a computer program that has beaten all of the best human players. It even developed new winning strategies that had never been seen in the long history of games.

Since then, the same type of technology has been used in many different fields. Do you have a personal assistant like Alexa or Siri? Do you get recommendations for movies you might enjoy on Netflix or products you might want to buy on Amazon? As we use these services, they learn what we like and get better at delivering things we want. People can chat with AI-powered chatbots online as if they're speaking to humans; weather forecasts are more accurate as AI systems analyze weather patterns; new medicines can be developed quickly and effectively thanks to AI. AI is even analyzing our art and creating new paintings, composing new music, and writing new stories!

However, there are also problems with AI. If the data that AI machines learn from is inaccurate, incomplete, or biased, the results will be, too. This could affect people's lives seriously if AI is being used to make decisions on borrowing money from the bank, treating patients in the hospital, or grading exams at universities. It's clear that AI is a powerful tool, but we need humans to check the results of AI in case things go wrong. And what about AI machines taking over the world? Well, it's not possible at the moment. AI is currently being used for very specific tasks, and although mistakes can happen, it's a pretty small risk. However, many scientists believe that by the end of the century, we will have superintelligent machines — machines that are more intelligent than we are. What if these machines come to the conclusion that the world would be better without humans? Perhaps we should take a look at those old science-fiction movies again!
