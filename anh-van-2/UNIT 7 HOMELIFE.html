THE CITY THAT BANNED CARS

I lived in the Spanish city of Pontevedra from 1990 to 1996. I remember the traffic jams were terrible, and the sidewalks were always full of parked cars and scooters. It was very noisy and dirty ... that's why I left! Now I'm here again, and it's completely different. There are green spaces and children's playgrounds everywhere. And I can't see any cars! I stop a man riding a bike slowly in a bike lane and ask, "This is so peaceful. How long has Pontevedra been like this?"

"We haven't had cars here for a long time", he replies with a smile. This is because when Miguel Anxo Fernández Lores became mayor of the city in 1999, he decided to ban almost all cars in the downtown area. Pontevedra has changed a lot since the ban. For many years, the traffic was very dangerous, and from 1996 to 2006, 30 people died in accidents, but nobody has died since 2009. There is less pollution, too. CO2 emissions have gone down 70%. Lores has been mayor for over twenty years, and he is very proud of the change in his city. "Public space belongs to everyone. When we walk, we are all equal", he says.

In addition to banning cars, the mayor doesn't allow any large shopping malls outside the downtown area. This means that everyone does his or her shopping in town. In many cities, local stores have closed down recently, but in Pontevedra, the city's stores have been busy for years.

These are not the only positive results of the changes in Pontevedra. For a long time, people saw the city as dirty and boring, and nobody wanted to live there. But since the ban, central Pontevedra has gained 12,000 new residents. Many of these new people are young families from other parts of Spain who want their children to live in a cleaner and safer city.

THE CUBO HOUSE:
A NEW SOLUTION TO
AN OLD PROBLEM

Around the world, millions of people live in poor-quality, crowded housing known as slums. People who live in slums have a low quality of life, and they are more likely to suffer from a variety of health problems. Governments would like to help these people, but building good-quality houses takes a long time and is expensive.

However, one young man has designed a house that may partly solve the problem. Earl Forlales is just 23 years old and from Manila, in the Philippines. His house, which he has called Cubo, is made from bamboo, and it has won first prize in a competition to develop cities of the future.

The judges particularly liked Cubo because it is so cheap and quick to build. In fact, Forlales says that the materials for a house can be made in one week, and it takes just four hours to build the house! Cubo is environmentally friendly, as well. Because bamboo is the fastest-growing plant in the world, these houses are very good for the planet, too!

The prize from the competition is $60,000 and Forlales is using that money to build models of the Cubo houses. He then hopes that companies will invest money in his business so he can begin building houses for the poorest people in Manila. He has already chosen an area of land, and he plans to build houses for 10,000 families in the Philippines in the next five years. Some of the houses will be connected to each other, so residents will share bathrooms and kitchens and, therefore, save money. Forlales hopes that this use of shared space will also help to build communities. Although he will only build Cubo in the Philippines at first, he believes the idea could also work in other parts of the world where bamboo grows, such as Africa and South America.

7.1  Present perfect with already and yet, and simple past with just

We use the simple past with just to talk about something that happened a short time ago:

Are you hungry?   No, I'm not. I just ate.

We use the present perfect with already to show that something happened before the expected time:

Should I load the dishwasher?   It's OK. I've already done it.

We use the present perfect with yet in negative sentences to talk about something that hasn't happened at the time of speaking, but will happen in the future:

Is lunch ready?   No, I haven't made it yet.

We use the present perfect with yet in questions to ask about something we expected to happen:

Have you washed the car yet?   No, I'll do it now.   ▶ 7.1

7.2  Present perfect with for and since

We use the present perfect to talk about the duration of a situation that started in the past and continues into the present:

How long have you lived in Mexico?
I've lived here all my life.

We use the present perfect with for to talk about a period of time and with since to talk about a starting point:

How long has she known David?
She's known him for six months.
She's known him since last December.

In addition to years, months, etc., we can use for and since with other words and phrases:

I've known Carmen for a long time / ages.
She's lived there since she got married / her wedding.

We use the simple past to talk about the duration of completed situations in the past:

How long did you live in Madrid?
I lived in Madrid for ten years.
How long have you lived in Tokyo?
I've lived in Tokyo since 2020.   ▶ 7.2

7.3  Linkers

We use linkers to connect ideas. We can use them to join two sentences, or to show how one sentence relates to another:

My new phone wasn't expensive. It takes great pictures. The battery doesn't last long.
My new phone wasn't expensive, and it takes great pictures. However, the battery doesn't last long.

We use the linkers and, as well, also, and too to add information:

Everyone in our apartment pays rent. We also share the bills.
The park is really nice. It's across from the office, too.

We use the linkers but, although, and however to contrast information:

Sharing an apartment is really fun, but it's sometimes pretty noisy.
Although my family lives near me, I don't see my parents very often. I love living in San Francisco. However, it's very expensive.   ▶ 7.3

A model city

Over the years, many people have called Curitiba a model city because it has become an example for other cities to follow. When the Brazilian city started to grow in the 1960s, town planners, including Jaime Lerner (who later became the mayor of Curitiba), wanted to create a green, friendly city where everything worked well together, while keeping the city's identity.

In 1972, they made part of the downtown area for pedestrians only, and in 1974, they developed the Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, with special bus lanes and bus stations on all the main roads in the city. They used longer buses and made it possible for people to pay before they got on, so trips were quicker, and the buses could carry more people. Today, about 85% of people in Curitiba use the BRT, and pollution in Curitiba is 25% lower than in other Brazilian cities.

Curitiba also has more than 50 square meters of parks and green space for each person who lives there – a total of 400 square kilometers! This compares to just 2 square meters of green space per person in Buenos Aires, Argentina. In addition, Curitiba has an excellent way of dealing with garbage. In 1989, the "trash for tokens" program began. It encourages people to recycle and also keep their neighborhoods clean and neat. For every 1.8 kg, of garbage they collect, they receive a token to buy 0.9 kg, of food. Today, more than 100,000 people use the "trash for tokens" system.

Although Curitiba sounds like a perfect city, it also has problems. Since the 1960s, the population has grown from 350,000 to about 2 million. There aren't enough homes that people can afford to buy, and so about 8% of the population lives in very poor-quality housing, called favelas.

AUDIO WB 7.7 Bilbao lecture — architecture

Look at this picture. Do you recognize the city? Anyone? It’s actually the city of Bilbao in the Basque Country region of northern Spain. Today, it’s a popular tourist destination. However, that wasn’t always the case. About 30 years ago, many people thought Bilbao was a gray, industrial city.

We are going to look at how the city has changed since the 1990s, and one of the main reasons for the amazing transformation is architecture. The most obvious example of this is probably the Guggenheim Museum, which was designed by Frank Gehry and has turned Bilbao into a totally different city. I think it’s one of the greatest buildings of the 20th century. Its modern design is spectacular, and it looks beautiful as the sun sets over the water.

And when visitors arrive in Bilbao, the very first thing they see is another impressive building. The passenger terminal at Bilbao Airport opened in the year 2000, and it was designed by Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava. Again, it’s a very modern building, and it reminds me of a bird with its wings stretched wide. What do you think?

You might think that public transportation is very dull. But the new metro system, designed by the British architect Norman Foster and opened in 1997, was equally important in the transformation of Bilbao. The goal was to build a metro near street level, not too deep, and with high ceilings to give a feeling of openness. The glass entrances are amazing. They’re called Fosteritos after their designer, and during the day, lots of sunlight pours in. So inside, passengers find the stations open and inviting. Foster’s metro makes people want to take public transportation in Bilbao.

AUDIO WB 7.8 Econduce & Plan Bici

Some of the largest cities in the world are in Latin America. Millions and millions of people live close together, and they all have to travel, whether it’s to work, to school, to go shopping, or to see friends and family. And all these trips cause traffic and pollution. Today, I want to look at two Latin American cities that are trying to solve these problems and have programs that help people travel around the city quickly and cleanly, without adding to the pollution.

First, I want to look at a program called Econduce, which people are using in Mexico City. Econduce is a network of electric scooters in the downtown area. Customers pay a monthly fee that includes everything they need for the scooters, such as helmets, insurance, and recharging the batteries. When they want to travel somewhere, they use an app to reserve a scooter nearby, and then they drive it to where they want to go. It’s easy to use, cheap, and clean. It was set up by Eduardo Porta and Alejandro Morales in 2015 with just 50 scooters. Today, there are hundreds of scooters and thousands of customers, so it’s been a big success.

Next, we’re going to look at Bogotá, Colombia. Bike riding has been popular in this city for a long time, but the mayor of the city has started a project called Plan Bici. It’s a network of bike lanes all over the city. It includes the 25-kilometer-long Quinto Centenario bikeway, which goes from the north of the city to south, and is just for bike riders, so they can travel quickly, safely, and cleanly, away from the buses and cars and other traffic. The aim is to have 10% of people in the city traveling by bike, which is great for the environment and also a fantastic way to stay in shape.
